\section{Mechanism Design}
\label{sec:mech}

This chapter formalizes the incentive layer of AgentTrust. Whereas the
identity--trust--accountability (ITA) architecture of the preceding chapter
describes \emph{what} the protocol records and \emph{how} it holds agents
accountable, here we make precise \emph{why} rational agents choose to behave
honestly. The core object of study is the \emph{Schelling-point reputation
community}: a set of staked voters who adjudicate trade disputes, whose verdicts
drive both the release of escrowed funds and the updating of on-chain
reputation, and whose reputation scores in turn price the very guarantees that
make future trades safe. The mechanism is instantiated by four production
Solidity contracts -- \textsf{AgentRegistry},
\textsf{GuaranteeEscrow}, \textsf{SchellingVoting}, and
\textsf{ReputationHub} -- and every parameter cited below matches the deployed
bytecode exactly.

We proceed as follows. Section~\ref{sec:goals} states the design goals and the
adversarial model, and fixes the participants, strategy space, and utility
functions used throughout. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} gives the formal
definition of the mechanism. Section~\ref{sec:analysis} presents the
game-theoretic analysis: honest voting as a best response (Lemma~\ref{lem:best-response}),
incentive compatibility (Theorem~\ref{thm:incentive}), collusion resistance
(Theorem~\ref{thm:collusion}), Sybil resistance (Theorem~\ref{thm:sybil}), and
deterministic fund safety (Lemma~\ref{lem:conservation}), together with a
quantitative justification of the two-thirds supermajority rule. Section
\ref{sec:pricing} closes the reputation--guarantee pricing loop, and
Section~\ref{sec:related} contrasts the design with existing decentralized
adjudication and attestation systems.

\subsection{Design Goals and Threat Model}
\label{sec:goals}
% ============================================================================

\subsubsection*{Participants.}
Let $\mathcal{I} = \{1, \dots, n\}$ be the set of registered \emph{agents}.
Each agent $i \in \mathcal{I}$ holds a non-fungible identity issued by
\textsf{AgentRegistry}; the registry binds $i$ to a unique on-chain principal
$o(i) \in \mathbb{A}$ (the entity legally responsible for $i$'s actions), so
every act by an agent is attributable to a real-world principal
\cite{erc8004}. Three roles interact within the mechanism:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Traders} -- a buyer agent $b$ and a seller agent $s$ who enter
        into a guaranteed commerce transaction of value $x$ (in wei).
  \item \emph{Guarantors} -- principals who post capital to insure the buyer
        against seller default, in exchange for a premium.
  \item \emph{Voters} -- community members who stake capital and vote on
        disputed outcomes; their collective verdict settles the case.
\end{enumerate}
The trader and guarantor roles are exercised through
\textsf{GuaranteeEscrow}; the voter role through \textsf{SchellingVoting}. All
write access to reputation is mediated by \textsf{ReputationHub}, which
maintains an access-control list of authorized callers (the escrow and voting
contracts), thereby precluding self-attestation by construction.

\subsubsection*{Design goals.}
We seek a mechanism satisfying five properties.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{G1}]\textbf{(Decentralized adjudication).} No single authority
        determines the winner of a dispute; outcomes are decided by a staked
        community supermajority. The platform operator retains no privileged
        arbitration role beyond bootstrapping (opening cases in the MVP).
  \item[\textbf{G2}]\textbf{(Incentive compatibility).} Truthful voting is a
        (strict) Bayesian--Nash equilibrium: given that the community converges
        on the true state, no voter can profit by misreporting her private
        information.
  \item[\textbf{G3}]\textbf{(Sybil resistance).} Neither identities nor votes
        can be multiplied for free: identity creation is gated by a
        registration stake, and every vote requires capital lock-up.
  \item[\textbf{G4}]\textbf{(Collusion resistance).} A coordinated minority
        cannot overturn the honest majority's verdict; forcing an outcome
        requires a strict two-thirds supermajority of capital-weighted votes.
  \item[\textbf{G5}]\textbf{(Deterministic fund safety).} At every state of the
        lifecycle, funds are conserved: the sum of deposits equals the sum of
        distributable value up to a bounded integer-division dust term, and no
        state transition can lock or leak value.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Threat model.}
We assume a fully rational adversary who controls a bounded fraction of
registered agents and/or of staked voting capital, and who may deviate
arbitrarily (computationally unbounded in strategy, economically budgeted in
capital). The protocol must remain secure against the following attack classes,
which specialize the abstract ITA threat catalogue of the preceding chapter.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{T1}]\textbf{(Sybil).} An adversary creates many fake agent
        identities and/or voting addresses to dominate adjudication or to
        inflate reputation. This is the classic Sybil attack
        \cite{douceur}; we bound it with registration stakes, per-vote capital
        lock-up, and a minimum quorum.
  \item[\textbf{T2}]\textbf{(Reputation manipulation).} An adversary rates
        herself (self-rating), colludes to rate conspirators, or otherwise
        inflates a reputation profile. We exclude self-rating by ACL-gated
        writes, and make ratings costly by routing all writes through the
        escrow/voting contracts where the write is driven by a staked verdict
        or an escrowed outcome.
  \item[\textbf{T3}]\textbf{(Delinquency and exit).} An agent earns a series of
        high-value trades, defaults once, and abandons the identity to relaunch
        under a fresh one (\emph{reputation laundering}). We bound this by the
        non-recoverable registration stake, by the permanence of the on-chain
        record, and by reputation-gated guarantee pricing that prices a fresh
        identity at the uninsurable default rate (Section~\ref{sec:pricing}).
  \item[\textbf{T4}]\textbf{(Single-point authority abuse).} Any participant
        who holds exclusive arbitration or write authority can extract rent or
        mis-route funds. The design minimizes such points: in the full
        protocol, the arbitration authority is exercised by
        \textsf{SchellingVoting} (community supermajority), not by a
        privileged key.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Strategy space and utility.}
Fix a disputed case $C$. Each voter $i$ chooses an action
$a_i \in \mathcal{A} = \{B, S, \bot\}$, where $B$ (BUYER), $S$ (SELLER), and
$\bot$ (ABSTAIN) denote the sides of the dispute she supports; choosing $a_i$
requires depositing a stake $v > 0$ wei (\textsf{SchellingVoting.vote} enforces
$\texttt{msg.value} = v$ exactly). Voter utility is quasi-linear,
$u_i(a) = P_i(a) - v$, where $P_i$ is the settlement payment defined in
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}. We model information with a two-point state space
$\theta \in \{B, S\}$ (the true party at fault); each voter receives a private
signal $\sigma_i \in \{B,S\}$ with accuracy
$q = \Pr[\sigma_i = \theta \mid \theta] > 1/2$, conditionally independent across
voters. This is the standard Condorcet jury information structure
\cite{condorcet}, which we instantiate under a staked-payment payoff in place of
the classical "correct decision" utility.

\subsection{Formal Mechanism Definition}
\label{sec:mechanism}
% ============================================================================

We now define the mechanism formally. Throughout, monetary quantities are
denominated in wei; ``$\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$'' is integer floor; all divisions
are integer divisions exactly as performed by the EVM.

\begin{definition}[Dispute case]
\label{def:case}
A \emph{dispute case} is a tuple
\[
C \;=\; (\tau,\ b,\ s,\ v,\ t_{d},\ m_{B},\ m_{S},\ m_{\bot},\ w,\ e),
\]
where $\tau$ is the trade identifier (\texttt{tradeId}), $b, s$ are the buyer
and seller agent identifiers, $v > 0$ is the per-vote stake (\texttt{stake}),
$t_{d}$ is the voting deadline (a block timestamp), $(m_{B}, m_{S}, m_{\bot})$
are the numbers of votes cast for BUYER, SELLER, and ABSTAIN respectively,
$w \in \{B, S, \bot\}$ is the winner (with $w = \bot$ denoting a void case),
and $e \in \{0,1\}$ is the effectiveness flag. The protocol constrains a case
by: (i) one vote per address (\texttt{hasVoted} is a per-case flag); (ii) votes
accepted only during the open window $(\cdot,\ t_{d})$; and (iii) at most one
case per trade (\texttt{tradeHasCase} prevents duplicate litigation), opened
only while the underlying trade is in the \texttt{DISPUTED} escrow state.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Voting game]
\label{def:game}
Given a case $C$, the induced \emph{voting game} is
\[
\Gamma_{C} = \big(\mathcal{N},\ \mathcal{A},\ S,\ P,\ u\big),
\]
where $\mathcal{N}$ is the set of eligible voters, $\mathcal{A} = \{B,S,\bot\}$
is the pure-action space, $S$ is the deposit schedule requiring exactly $v$ wei
per vote, $P$ is the settlement payment function of
Definition~\ref{def:settlement}, and $u_{i}(a) = P_{i}(a) - v$ is the
quasi-linear utility of voter $i$. A mixed strategy of voter $i$ is a
distribution over $\mathcal{A}$; the Bayesian game extends $\Gamma_{C}$ with
the type space of signals $(\sigma_{i})$ and the common prior over
$(\theta, \sigma_{1}, \dots, \sigma_{|\mathcal{N}|})$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Adjudication rule]
\label{def:rule}
Let $T = m_{B} + m_{S}$ be the number of \emph{effective} votes (abstentions are
excluded). The adjudication rule $R$ maps the vote profile to
$(w, e)$ as follows, with $\kappa = \texttt{MAJORITY\_BPS}/10000 = 0.65$ and
$\nu = \texttt{MIN\_VOTERS} = 3$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item If $T = 0$, set $w = \bot$ and $e = 0$ (no votes cast; the contract
        short-circuits the majority test, which would otherwise vacuously hold
        because $0 \ge 0$).
  \item Otherwise, for each side $X \in \{B, S\}$ define the supermajority
        predicate
        \[
        \mathsf{maj}(X) \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
        m_{X} \cdot 10000 \;\ge\; T \cdot \texttt{MAJORITY\_BPS},
        \]
        and set
        $w = B$ if $\mathsf{maj}(B)$, else $w = S$ if $\mathsf{maj}(S)$, else
        $w = \bot$.
  \item Finally,
        $e = 1 \Longleftrightarrow \big(T \ge \nu \ \wedge\ w \neq \bot\big)$;
        otherwise $e = 0$ and the case is \emph{void}.
\end{enumerate}
Because $\kappa = 0.65 > 1/2$, the two predicates $\mathsf{maj}(B)$ and
$\mathsf{maj}(S)$ cannot both hold for any $T \ge 1$; the rule is therefore
well-defined. Note that $\kappa = 0.65$ is an integer-safe engineering
approximation of the exact two-thirds threshold $\tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.6667$
(Section~\ref{sec:threshold}); the contract's comment documents this choice as
avoiding boundary integer-division ambiguity.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Settlement payments]
\label{def:settlement}
Let $w$ be the winner and $e$ the effectiveness of a settled case. Let
$m_{w} = m_{B}$ if $w = B$, else $m_{w} = m_{S}$; let $m_{\ell} = T - m_{w}$.
The settlement payment function $P$ is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Effective case} ($e = 1$). Each majority voter receives
        \[
        P_{i} = v + \left\lfloor \frac{v \cdot m_{\ell}}{m_{w}} \right\rfloor,
        \]
        i.e., her principal plus a pro-rata share of the forfeited minority
        pool ($\texttt{claimReward}$, $T \ge \nu$ guaranteed). Each minority
        voter receives $P_{i} = 0$ (her stake is forfeited). Each ABSTAIN
        voter receives $P_{i} = v$ ($\texttt{claimRefund}$).
  \item \textbf{Void case} ($e = 0$). Every voter -- majority, minority, or
        abstainer -- receives $P_{i} = v$; no rewards are distributed.
\end{enumerate}
The integer-division remainder $\delta = (v \cdot m_{\ell}) \bmod m_{w}$
remains in the contract; it is bounded by $\delta < m_{w}$ wei and is a known,
documented limitation of the MVP (a batched-settlement refinement in the full
design removes it).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Escrow linkage]
\label{def:escrow}
On settlement, the voting contract invokes the escrow's dispute-resolution
entry point,
\[
\texttt{escrow.resolveDispute}(\tau,\ \texttt{verdict},\ \texttt{share}),
\]
on the underlying trade $\tau$, which releases escrowed value and updates
reputation as follows. The escrow enforces the state machine
$\text{CREATED} \to \text{FUNDED} \to \text{GUARANTEED} \to \text{DELIVERED}
\to \text{DISPUTED} \to \text{RELEASED}/\text{RESOLVED}$, with four
one-day windows (funding, guarantee, delivery, and confirmation); a case can
be opened only while the trade is in the \texttt{DISPUTED} state
(Definition~\ref{def:case}).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item If $w = B$ (including the conservative default of a void case), the
        verdict is \texttt{BUYER\_WINS} with $\texttt{share} = 10000$. The
        buyer receives $\texttt{buyerRefund} + \texttt{stake}$, where
        $\texttt{buyerRefund} = \lfloor x \cdot \texttt{share}/10000 \rfloor$
        and $\texttt{stake} = \lfloor x \cdot \gamma / 10^{18} \rfloor$ is the
        guarantor's posted collateral; the seller receives the residual
        $x - \texttt{buyerRefund}$ if positive. The seller is recorded as
        \texttt{LOST}.
  \item If $w = S$, the verdict is \texttt{SELLER\_WINS} with
        $\texttt{share} = 0$. The seller receives $x - \pi$ (amount less the
        premium) and the guarantor receives $\texttt{stake} + \pi$; the seller
        is recorded as \texttt{WON}.
\end{enumerate}
The verdict thereby \emph{releases} escrowed value, and simultaneously feeds
the reputation record (Definition~\ref{def:rep}); neither can occur without the
other. All amounts are verified to sum to the escrow's holding $x + \texttt{stake}$
in every branch (Lemma~\ref{lem:conservation}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Reputation update rule]
\label{def:rep}
\textsf{ReputationHub} maintains, per agent, a four-dimensional behavior
profile $\rho = (c, d, w, l)$ counting \texttt{COMPLETED}, \texttt{DEFAULTED},
\texttt{WON}, and \texttt{LOST} outcomes, written exclusively by authorized
callers (the escrow and voting contracts). From the seller's perspective: a
trade confirmed or auto-released records \texttt{COMPLETED}; a seller default
on the delivery window records \texttt{DEFAULTED}; a dispute won or lost
records \texttt{WON} or \texttt{LOST}. The scalar score is
\[
\mathsf{score}(\rho) \;=\;
100 \;-\; 100 \cdot \frac{d}{\theta} \;-\; 50 \cdot \frac{l}{\theta},
\qquad \theta = c + d + w + l,
\]
clipped to $[0, 100]$, and $\mathsf{score} = 50$ for a new agent with
$\theta = 0$. A default reduces the score by up to $100$; a lost dispute by up
to $50$; completed trades and won disputes never reduce it.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Game-Theoretic Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
% ============================================================================

We analyze the mechanism in three parts: the per-voter incentive to tell the
truth, the system-level barriers to coalition and Sybil attacks, and the
fund-safety invariant. Throughout, we fix a case $C$ with stake $v$, and let
$h$ (resp.\ $c$) denote the number of votes cast by honest (resp.\ adversarial)
voters.

\paragraph{Signal structure.}
Each honest voter $i$ receives signal $\sigma_{i}$ with
$\Pr[\sigma_{i} = \theta \mid \theta] = q > 1/2$, conditionally independent
across voters. Honest voters are assumed to be rational and to coordinate on
the \emph{truthful} Schelling point: they vote the side they believe is at
fault, because the majority side collects the minority's forfeited stakes
\cite{schelling,truthful-eq}. We first show that this behavior is indeed a best
response to honest others.

\begin{lemma}[Honest voting is a best response]
\label{lem:best-response}
Fix a voter $i$ holding signal $\sigma_{i}$ with accuracy $q > 1/2$, and
suppose every other voter votes her own signal. Then voting $\sigma_{i}$
strictly maximizes $i$'s expected settlement payoff among $\{B, S\}$, and
strictly dominates abstention whenever the other voters carry the correct side
to a supermajority. Concretely,
\[
\mathbb{E}[u_{i} \mid a_{i} = \sigma_{i}]
\;-\; \mathbb{E}[u_{i} \mid a_{i} = \bar\sigma_{i}]
\;=\;
v \left(1 + \frac{m_{\ell}}{m_{w}}\right) \big(p_{\sigma_{i}} - p_{\bar\sigma_{i}}\big)
\;>\; 0,
\]
where $p_{X}$ is the probability that voter $i$ is in the majority when voting
$X$, and $p_{\sigma_{i}} - p_{\bar\sigma_{i}} \ge (2q - 1) - \varepsilon_{n}$,
with $\varepsilon_{n} \to 0$ as the number of voters grows.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The deposit $v$ is sunk at the time of the vote, so $i$ maximizes her expected
settlement payoff. Let $m_{B}, m_{S}$ denote the counts of the \emph{other}
voters' votes, $m_{B} + m_{S} = n - 1$. If $i$ votes $B$, the effective totals
become $(m_{B} + 1, m_{S})$; by rule $R$ she is in the majority iff
$m_{B} + 1 \ge \kappa\,(m_{B} + m_{S} + 1)$. If she votes $S$, she is in the
majority iff $m_{S} + 1 \ge \kappa\,(m_{B} + m_{S} + 1)$. Except on the
one-vote-wide pivotal region where these two inequalities differ, the winner is
independent of $i$'s vote and $p_{B} = p_{S}$; the entire payoff gap is
concentrated on the pivot event.

Conditional on the true state, the other honest votes are conditionally i.i.d.\
with success probability $q$, hence
$m_{B} \mid \theta \! = \! B \sim \mathsf{Bin}(n-1, q)$
first-order stochastically dominates
$m_{B} \mid \theta \! = \! S \sim \mathsf{Bin}(n-1, 1-q)$.
Because $i$'s own signal satisfies $\Pr[\theta = \sigma_{i}] = q > 1/2$, she
weights the correct-state branch more heavily, giving
$p_{B} > p_{S}$ when $\sigma_{i} = B$ (and symmetrically). Concretely, the
probability that her vote is pivotal and decisive for the truthful side exceeds
the analogous probability for the untruthful side by at least $2q - 1$, up to
the vanishing slack $\varepsilon_{n}$ from the law of large numbers. By
Definition~\ref{def:settlement}, her expected net payoff under vote $X$ is
$v\big(p_{X}(1 + m_{\ell}/m_{w}) - 1\big)$, so the gap above equals
$v(1 + m_{\ell}/m_{w})(p_{B} - p_{S}) > 0$. For abstention: if the other voters
already carry the correct side to a supermajority, voting one's signal yields a
strictly positive expected net payoff (the pivot bonus), whereas abstention
yields exactly $0$; abstention is therefore strictly dominated.\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Incentive compatibility: truthful voting is an equilibrium]
\label{thm:incentive}
Let every voter's signal accuracy satisfy $q > 1/2$, and suppose the honest
community commands at least a fraction $\kappa = 0.65$ of the effective votes
in expectation. Then the profile in which every voter votes her signal is a
strict Bayesian--Nash equilibrium: no voter can strictly improve by any
unilateral deviation. Moreover, the resulting verdict coincides with the true
state with probability converging to $1$ as the number of voters grows, and the
truthful profile is the unique \emph{signal-sustained} Schelling point among
the coordination equilibria of $\Gamma_{C}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{(Equilibrium.)} Take any voter $i$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:best-response},
given that all other voters vote their signals, every $a_{i} \in \{B,S,\bot\}$
with $a_{i} \neq \sigma_{i}$ yields strictly lower expected utility than
$a_{i} = \sigma_{i}$ (abstention being dominated whenever the honest
supermajority materializes, which occurs with probability $1 - \varepsilon$ by
the assumption that the honest block commands a $\ge \kappa$ fraction). Hence
no unilateral deviation is profitable and the inequality is strict, so the
truthful profile is a strict Bayesian--Nash equilibrium.

\textbf{(Correctness.)} Conditional on $\theta = B$, the honest votes are i.i.d.\
with success probability $q > 1/2$, so by the law of large numbers the fraction
of B votes among honest voters tends to $q$. Since the honest block commands at
least $\kappa = 0.65$ of all effective votes and $q > \kappa$ is not required
for this step -- the block simply needs its own supermajority to exceed
$\kappa$ -- the B share of total effective votes exceeds $\kappa$ with
probability tending to $1$; hence $R$ returns $w = B$ with probability tending
to $1$. The argument for $\theta = S$ is symmetric.

\textbf{(Schelling point.)} Other coordination profiles (e.g., all voters
choosing $B$ irrespective of signals) are Nash equilibria as well, since no
single voter can change the outcome if the rest coordinate. But any such
coordination equilibrium is uncorrelated with the true state: it rewards the
majority regardless of the merits. The truthful profile is the unique
equilibrium in which (i) each voter's action is a best response to a signal,
(ii) the outcome is statistically correct, and (iii) expected payoffs are
strictly higher conditional on any realization where the coordinated and
truthful winners differ (the truthful majority collects a forfeiture bonus,
whereas the mis-coordinated majority gains nothing real -- the escrow still
releases value to the wrong party only for a single case). Following
Schelling's focal-point theory, self-interested voters with a common prior
converge on the signal-sustained profile because it is the unique one
justifiable to the community \cite{schelling,truthcoin,truthful-eq}.\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Scope of the dominance claim]
\label{rem:scope}
Lemma~\ref{lem:best-response} establishes honesty as a strict best response to
honest others, not as a dominant strategy against a fully adversarial electorate
(a lone honest voter against $n-1$ adversarial votes cannot force the truth).
This is the standard truthful-equilibrium property of Schelling-style staked
voting \cite{truthcoin,truthful-eq}; the \emph{system-level} protection against
adversarial electorates is exactly the quantitative barrier of
Theorem~\ref{thm:collusion} and Theorem~\ref{thm:sybil}.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Collusion resistance]
\label{thm:collusion}
Let $h$ honest and $c$ adversarial voters participate in a case, with
$n_{e} = h + c$ effective votes. To force a verdict contrary to the will of the
$h$ honest voters, the coalition must command
\[
c \;\ge\; \left\lceil \frac{13}{7}\, h \right\rceil
\;\approx\; 1.857\, h
\qquad(\text{i.e., } c \ge 2h \text{ under the ideal threshold } \tfrac{2}{3}),
\]
effective votes, i.e., a strict supermajority of at least $0.65\, n_{e}$.
Consequently: (i) any coalition of at most $35\%$ of the effective voters is
strategically harmless -- it can neither determine nor veto an outcome, and its
stakes are forfeited; and (ii) the minimum capital an attacker must lock is
$c \cdot v \ge \lceil 13h/7 \rceil \cdot v$, and a rational attacker will not
mount an attack when this exceeds the recoverable dispute value
($c \cdot v > x + \pi$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For the coalition's preferred side $X$ to win, rule $R$ (Definition~\ref{def:rule})
requires $m_{X} \ge \kappa\, T$. In the best case for the coalition, every
honest voter opposes it, so $m_{X} = c$ and $T = c + h$; the binding inequality
is $c \ge 0.65\,(c + h)$, which rearranges to $c \ge \tfrac{13}{7}h$. If the
coalition fails this bound, the honest side either carries the case (when
$h \ge \tfrac{13}{7}c$) or the case is void (when neither side reaches $0.65$);
in the latter event the conservative default releases the escrow to the buyer
(Section~\ref{sec:default}), never to the coalition's preferred verdict against
an honest block. Hence the coalition's \emph{only} path to a dictated verdict
requires the supermajority of $0.65\,n_{e}$, i.e., $c \ge \lceil 13h/7 \rceil$.
Claim (i) follows because any $c \le 0.35\,n_{e}$ fails the bound; such votes
join a losing minority and are forfeited by Definition~\ref{def:settlement}.
Claim (ii) follows from the capital lock: the attacker risks $c \cdot v$ wei
(plus the registration cost of Theorem~\ref{thm:sybil}), and the total value
extractable from a captured verdict is at most the trade value plus premium,
$x + \pi$. When $c \cdot v > x + \pi$, expected attack profit is strictly
negative for any win probability bounded away from $1$ -- and by the barrier
above it is not $1$ unless $c$ meets the supermajority, at which point the cost
dominates.\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Void-case vetoing]
\label{rem:veto}
A coalition that cannot reach $0.65$ can still \emph{veto} an honest verdict by
preventing the honest side from reaching $\kappa$; but this requires diluting
the effective total with abstentions or adversarial votes to force a void. Since
void cases default to the buyer and refund every stake, the veto yields the
coalition at most the conservative buyer-wins outcome, never a seller-favorable
verdict against an honest block. Moreover, inducing honest voters to abstain
requires compensating them for the positive expected bonus of
Lemma~\ref{lem:best-response}, adding a bribe cost to the attack. The veto
therefore cannot subvert the buyer-insurance guarantee that motivates the
escrow.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Sybil resistance]
\label{thm:sybil}
Let an adversary create $k$ Sybil identities. Each identity costs
$\texttt{registrationFee}$ wei to register in \textsf{AgentRegistry} and is
permanently bound to a real principal; each effective vote additionally
requires a capital lock of $v$ wei in \textsf{SchellingVoting}; and no case can
be decided by fewer than $\texttt{MIN\_VOTERS} = 3$ effective votes. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:collusion}, capturing a case against $h \ge 1$ honest voters
requires
\[
k \;\ge\; \left\lceil \frac{13}{7}\,h \right\rceil,
\]
so the minimum attack expenditure is at least
$\lceil 13h/7 \rceil \cdot (v + \texttt{registrationFee})$ wei. Because
identity creation and voting both demand real capital that is non-reusable while
locked, adversarial voting power scales \emph{linearly} in expenditure, and the
quorum $\nu = 3$ rules out one- or two-identity Sybils deciding a case.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Three protocol constraints jointly bound Sybil power. First,
\textsf{AgentRegistry.registerAgent} requires a payment of at least
$\texttt{registrationFee}$ and binds the new identity to the caller, so each
Sybil identity is a paid, attributable liability rather than a free vote.
Second, \textsf{SchellingVoting.vote} requires the exact per-vote deposit
$\texttt{msg.value} = v$ and permits one vote per address, so each effective
Sybil vote locks a full unit of capital that cannot be reused until the case
settles. Third, rule $R$ requires $T \ge \nu = 3$ effective votes for a case to
be effective, so a Sybil of size one or two cannot manufacture a verdict at all.
Combining these with Theorem~\ref{thm:collusion}, which mandates
$c \ge \lceil 13h/7 \rceil$ to capture a case, yields the stated expenditure
bound. Linearity follows because neither the identity cost nor the vote deposit
can be amortized across identities while locked.\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[MVP vs.\ full design]
\label{rem:sybil-mvp}
The MVP's \textsf{SchellingVoting} enforces the \emph{capital} Sybil barrier
(exact per-vote stake, one vote per address, $\nu = 3$); the identity barrier
(the requirement that voters hold registered agent IDs) is enforced by the
escrow/trading path and is a stated refinement of the full protocol, which
binds every vote to a registered, fee-paid identity. Both barriers are needed
for the full bound; the capital barrier alone already prevents cost-free
vote multiplication.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Sufficiency, not exactness, of the Sybil bound]
\label{rem:sybil-sim}
The bound $k \ge \lceil 13h/7 \rceil$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:sybil} is a
\emph{sufficient} condition: it guarantees the coalition captures the case
(its preferred side reaches $\kappa$ with certainty). It is not the smallest
coalition that yields a \emph{high-probability} capture, because honest voters
err with probability $1 - q$ (Definition~\ref{def:case}), leaking a share of
honest votes to the adversary's side. Our Monte-Carlo evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}) confirms this: at $h = 14$ honest voters and
$q = 0.85$, the flip probability reaches $0.65$ already at $k = 22$, below
$\lceil 13h/7 \rceil = 26$, and certainty only at $k = 26$. The bound therefore
overstates the necessary adversarial expenditure in noisy electorates; the
exact capture set is the threshold boundary $m_{X} \ge \kappa\,T$ of
Definition~\ref{def:rule}, which the protocol enforces regardless of coalition
size. For the security claim this direction is the safe one: the mechanism
never needs \emph{more} adversarial capital to resist an attack than the bound
indicates.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Deterministic fund safety]
\label{lem:conservation}
For any case $C$, the total staked pool is $(m_{B} + m_{S} + m_{\bot}) \cdot v$
wei. The settlement payments of Definition~\ref{def:settlement} satisfy
\[
\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} P_{i}
\;=\;
\begin{cases}
(m_{B} + m_{S} + m_{\bot}) \cdot v - \delta, & e = 1,\ \delta = (v\, m_{\ell}) \bmod m_{w}, \\[2pt]
(m_{B} + m_{S} + m_{\bot}) \cdot v,          & e = 0,
\end{cases}
\]
with $0 \le \delta < m_{w}$. In every effective settlement the majority
reclaims its principal and divides the minority's forfeited stakes; in every
void settlement all stakes are refunded. Hence the voting contract never
over-disburses and never accumulates unaccounted value beyond the bounded dust
term $\delta$. At the escrow layer, the same invariant holds: the escrow holds
$x + \texttt{stake}$ wei and disburses exactly $x + \texttt{stake}$ across every
outcome (Table~\ref{tab:escrow-conservation}).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In an effective case ($e = 1$), $m_{w}$ majority voters each receive
$v + \lfloor v m_{\ell}/m_{w} \rfloor$, $m_{\ell}$ minority voters receive $0$,
and $m_{\bot}$ abstainers receive $v$. The total is
\[
m_{w}\left(v + \left\lfloor \tfrac{v m_{\ell}}{m_{w}} \right\rfloor\right)
+ m_{\bot} v
\;=\; m_{w} v + (v m_{\ell} - \delta) + m_{\bot} v
\;=\; (m_{B} + m_{S} + m_{\bot})v - \delta,
\]
since $m_{w} + m_{\ell} = m_{B} + m_{S}$. In a void case ($e = 0$), every voter
claims $v$, summing to the full pool with $\delta = 0$. The dust bound
$\delta = (v m_{\ell}) \bmod m_{w} < m_{w}$ follows from the integer remainder.
For the escrow: enumerating the outcome classes (success, seller wins, buyer
wins in full/partial, and the two refund paths) shows each distributes
$x + \texttt{stake}$ or, in the pre-guarantee refund, the full $x$ held at that
state; the premium $\pi$ is never deposited and is netted from the seller's
share, so it cannot be lost.\end{proof}

\paragraph{The conservative default on void cases.}
\label{sec:default}
When a case is void -- no votes, fewer than $\nu$ effective votes, or no side
reaching the supermajority -- rule $R$ returns $w = \bot$ and the escrow
\emph{conservatively defaults to the buyer}: verdict \texttt{BUYER\_WINS},
$\texttt{share} = 10000$ (Definition~\ref{def:escrow}). The buyer is refunded
her principal plus the guarantor's forfeited stake; the seller's claim to
payment is denied. This asymmetry is economically deliberate and not an
artifact:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Insurance semantics.} The guarantor has voluntarily accepted
        the role of insuring the buyer against seller default. When the
        community cannot establish the seller's claim to a supermajority, the
        seller's delivery claim is treated as unsubstantiated, and the
        insurance settlement is exercised in the buyer's favor -- the standard
        burden-of-proof allocation in which the party seeking payment must
        establish entitlement.
  \item \textbf{No value lock.} Funds are released deterministically rather
        than frozen; G5 holds unconditionally, including on void paths.
  \item \textbf{Guarantor discipline.} The default-buyer outcome makes a
        guarantor who underpriced a risky seller absorb the loss, aligning
        guarantee pricing (Section~\ref{sec:pricing}) with the community's
        future verdicts and deterring adverse selection into the guarantee
        market.
\end{enumerate}
Two caveats are documented. First, a void case still records a \texttt{LOST}
outcome for the seller through the default path, which is a deliberate MVP
simplification; the full protocol distinguishes \emph{void} from
\emph{adjudicated loss} so that a seller who merely faced an abandoned dispute
is not penalized. Second, the default gives a buyer who files a frivolous
dispute a ``win by default'' if the community fails to respond; this is bounded
because the honest community needs only $\nu = 3$ votes and a supermajority to
rule \texttt{SELLER\_WINS} (Lemma~\ref{lem:best-response} makes truthful
response a best response), and because repeated frivolous disputes are
themselves on-chain-visible and subject to the community's reputational
scrutiny.

\paragraph{Why a two-thirds supermajority?}
\label{sec:threshold}
The threshold $\kappa = \texttt{MAJORITY\_BPS}/10000 = 0.65$ is chosen for three
quantitative reasons, none of which hold at the bare-majority rule
$\kappa = 1/2$.

\emph{(a) Resisting a one-third malicious minority.} For any contested case, the
honest block wins iff its vote share exceeds $\kappa$. With $\kappa = 1/2$, an
adversary holding just over half of the votes wins every case it contests, and
the honest block must exceed half to survive. With $\kappa = 0.65$, an
adversary controlling up to $35\%$ of the effective votes can \emph{never}
determine an outcome (Theorem~\ref{thm:collusion}); the mechanism tolerates a
coordinated malicious minority of roughly one third while still producing the
correct verdict whenever honest voters command a $\ge 0.65$ share.

\emph{(b) Capping the required honest supermajority.} A threshold close to $1$
(unanimity) would render the mechanism fragile: a small honest abstention or a
little signal noise would void legitimate cases. At $\kappa = 0.65$, the honest
block needs only two thirds of the votes, so up to one third of the electorate
may abstain, err, or collude without voiding a valid verdict -- keeping the
truthful equilibrium of Theorem~\ref{thm:incentive} sustainable without
near-unanimity.

\emph{(c) Optimizing the voting incentive at the boundary.} The per-winner bonus
is $v \cdot m_{\ell}/m_{w}$, which at the decision boundary equals
$v \cdot (1-\kappa)/\kappa$: a $50\%$ return at $\kappa = 2/3$, $100\%$ at
$\kappa = 1/2$, and $33\%$ at $\kappa = 3/4$. The supermajority rule thus
preserves a strong participation bonus at the exact margin where a verdict is
contested, while the bare-majority rule's larger bonus buys no collusion
resistance at all.

The engineering constant $\texttt{MAJORITY\_BPS} = 6500$ is the integer-safe
approximation of $\tfrac{2}{3} \approx 6667/10000$; it lowers the adversarial
requirement marginally (factor $\tfrac{13}{7} \approx 1.857$ instead of $2$) at
negligible security cost, in exchange for exact integer arithmetic and a
slightly smaller void region near the threshold boundary.

The choice is borne out quantitatively by the Monte-Carlo evaluation of
Section~\ref{sec:eval}: scanning the threshold with $n = 15$ honest voters and
$q = 0.7$ yields a correct-verdict rate of $0.947$ at $\kappa = 0.5$, $0.721$
at $\kappa = 0.65$, and $0.512$ at $\kappa = 0.7$, while the void rate climbs
from $0$ to $0.275$ to $0.487$. The supermajority rule thus trades at most
$0.23$ of correctness for immunity to a one-third malicious minority, and
raising $\kappa$ beyond $0.65$ buys no further safety while voiding nearly half
of all cases. The same evaluation confirms the $35\%$ tolerance of reason (a)
directly: with $n = 21$ and $q = 0.85$, a coalition controlling $38\%$ of the
effective votes flips the verdict with probability below $1\%$, and certainty
is reached only at the $0.65$ supermajority boundary.

\subsection{The Reputation--Guarantee Pricing Loop}
\label{sec:pricing}
% ============================================================================

The design's central novelty is not adjudication per se but the \emph{closed
loop} that connects verdicts to future commerce: each settlement updates a
reputation profile, reputation prices the guarantee that makes the next trade
safe, and the guarantee determines whether low-reputation agents can transact
at all.

\begin{definition}[Guarantee contract and pricing]
\label{def:pricing}
A guarantee is a pair $(\gamma, \pi)$ with coverage $\gamma \in (0, 2]$ (fraction
of $x$, up to $200\%$) and premium $\pi \ge 0$ wei, subject to
$\pi \le x$ (\texttt{premium} $\le$ \texttt{amount}, enforced to prevent
underflow of the seller's share $x - \pi$). The guarantor deposits
$\texttt{stake} = \lfloor x \gamma / 10^{18} \rfloor$ wei into
\textsf{GuaranteeEscrow}. Let $p_{D}(\rho)$ be the probability that a seller with
profile $\rho$ defaults. A rational guarantor participates only if its expected
net payoff is non-negative:
\[
(1 - p_{D})\,\pi \;-\; p_{D}\,\texttt{stake} \;\ge\; 0
\;\Longleftrightarrow\;
\pi \;\ge\; \frac{p_{D}}{1 - p_{D}} \cdot \texttt{stake}.
\]
We call $\pi^{*}(\rho, \gamma) = \dfrac{p_{D}}{1 - p_{D}} \cdot \texttt{stake}$
the \emph{competitive premium floor}.
\end{definition}

To instantiate the floor we need an estimator of the default probability from
the on-chain profile. The natural choice is the empirical default rate
$\hat p_{D}(\rho) = d/\theta$, which is exactly the quantity the reputation
score is built on. Substituting and using the identity
$d/\theta = (100 - \mathsf{score}(\rho))/100 - l/(2\theta)$, a first-order,
monotone estimator is $\hat p_{D}(s) = (100 - s)/100$ for a score
$s = \mathsf{score}(\rho) \in [0, 100]$; it is a valid probability, equals $0$
at the maximal score, and equals $1$ at the minimal score.

\begin{proposition}[Monotone guarantee pricing]
\label{prop:pricing}
Fix a trade value $x$ and coverage $\gamma > 0$, and set
$\hat p_{D}(s) = (100 - s)/100$. The competitive premium floor
\[
\pi^{*}(s) \;=\; \frac{\hat p_{D}(s)}{1 - \hat p_{D}(s)} \cdot \texttt{stake}
\;=\; \texttt{stake} \cdot \frac{100 - s}{s}
\]
is strictly decreasing in the reputation score $s$ on $(0, 100]$:
$\dfrac{\partial \pi^{*}}{\partial s} = -\,100\,\texttt{stake}/s^{2} < 0$.
Consequently the seller's net proceeds $x - \pi^{*}(s)$ are strictly increasing
in $s$, and a guarantor who quotes $\pi < \pi^{*}(s)$ is strictly loss-making in
expectation, while any $\pi \ge \pi^{*}(s)$ yields non-negative expected payoff.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The estimator $\hat p_{D}(s)$ is strictly decreasing in $s$, and the floor
$\pi^{*} = \texttt{stake} \cdot \hat p_{D}/(1 - \hat p_{D})$ is strictly
increasing in $\hat p_{D}$ on $[0,1)$ (its derivative w.r.t.\ $\hat p_{D}$ is
$\texttt{stake}/(1-\hat p_{D})^{2} > 0$). The composition is therefore strictly
decreasing in $s$; explicitly,
$\tfrac{\partial \pi^{*}}{\partial s} = \texttt{stake} \cdot \tfrac{d}{ds}
\tfrac{100-s}{s} = -\tfrac{100 \,\texttt{stake}}{s^{2}} < 0$. The monotonicity
of $x - \pi^{*}(s)$ is immediate. The zero-profit condition
$\pi = \pi^{*}(s)$ makes the guarantor's expected payoff exactly $0$; any lower
premium yields negative expectation by Definition~\ref{def:pricing}.\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Pricing penalizes delinquency; honors reliability]
\label{cor:pricing}
Under Proposition~\ref{prop:pricing}, a seller whose score falls from $s_{1}$ to
$s_{2} < s_{1}$ (e.g., by a recorded \texttt{DEFAULTED} or \texttt{LOST}) faces
a strictly higher competitive premium, $\pi^{*}(s_{2}) > \pi^{*}(s_{1})$, and
hence strictly lower net revenue on every subsequent trade. At
$s \to 0$ the floor diverges, meaning a maximally bad seller cannot obtain
actuarially fair insurance at all; at the new-agent score $s = 50$
(\textsf{ReputationHub} default), the floor is exactly one stake per trade,
which is why the protocol requires a guarantor's endorsement for new agents to
trade.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Immediate from the strict monotonicity of Proposition~\ref{prop:pricing} and the
evaluation $\pi^{*}(50) = \texttt{stake}$, $\pi^{*}(0) \to \infty$,
$\pi^{*}(100) = 0$.\end{proof}

The closed loop is summarized by the cycle
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{trade outcome} &\;\to\; \text{reputation record} \;\to\; \text{score } s \\
&\;\to\; \text{guarantee price } \pi^{*}(s),\ \text{coverage requirement} \\
&\;\to\; \text{admission and next-trade cost} \;\to\; \text{incentive to perform}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Each arrow is a deterministic, on-chain-verifiable step: outcomes are recorded
by the escrow/voting contracts (Definition~\ref{def:rep}); the score is computed
on-chain by \textsf{ReputationHub}; the floor is a closed-form function of $s$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:pricing}); and the admission decision is enforced by the
guarantor market, which quotes $\pi \ge \pi^{*}(s)$ only for sellers whose
expected loss it can bear. The loop makes reputation a \emph{storable,
revenue-bearing} asset and squarely addresses the delinquency-and-exit threat
(T3): abandoning a depleted identity forfeits the registration stake and the
accumulated goodwill, while relaunching a fresh identity begins at $s = 50$
with an uninsured profile -- the uninsurable floor $\pi^{*}(s) \to \infty$ as
$s \to 0$ prices the worst offenders out of the guarantee market before they can
harm a first escrow.

\paragraph{Why the loop is necessary: the abstention trap.}
A voter who abstains forgoes the winning bonus but never suffers the loser's
forfeiture; because the MVP's \textsf{SchellingVoting} \emph{refunds}
abstainers (Definition~\ref{def:settlement}), the expected one-shot monetary
payoff of abstaining is exactly zero, while the payoff of honest voting is
positive but small -- the per-winner bonus $v \cdot m_{\ell}/m_{w}$ is divided
among a supermajority and shrinks as the electorate grows. In a purely
one-shot, purely monetary reading, an honest voter is therefore only marginally
better off than an abstainer. This is not a defect of the adjudication rule but
the well-known need for a reputational component in Schelling-style voting, and
it is precisely what the closed loop supplies: participation in adjudication is
observable, recorded in the profile, and priced into future guarantees, so that
repeated useful voting becomes a revenue-bearing reputation asset while
persistent abstention carries an opportunity cost (higher $\pi^{*}$, fewer trade
opportunities). The loop converts a one-shot indifference (vote vs.\ abstain)
into a repeated-game strict preference. Our simulation makes the premise
quantitative: at $q = 0.7$ and $q = 0.9$, the honest-voting payoff is
statistically indistinguishable from zero at the $95\%$ confidence level, so
the reputational tie-breaker is not optional but load-bearing for
participation (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\paragraph{Kleros \cite{kleros}.}
Kleros is the closest ancestor: it selects jurors by token-weighted lottery and
uses stake-backed majority voting with rewards for the majority and slashing for
the minority, an incentive structure closely related to ours. Its differences
are structural. First, Kleros draws a \emph{random} jury per case; AgentTrust
openly admits all community votes (with random sampling left as a refinement),
relying instead on the two-thirds supermajority and the quorum to stabilize
verdicts. Second -- and decisively for our contribution -- Kleros maintains no
reputation record of the disputing parties, and its verdicts do not flow back
into any pricing signal. AgentTrust's verdicts write \texttt{WON}/\texttt{LOST}
into \textsf{ReputationHub}, whose score is a closed-form input to the guarantee
premium (Proposition~\ref{prop:pricing}); the adjudication is thus
\emph{productive}, not terminal.

\paragraph{Aragon Court \cite{aragon}.}
Aragon Court adjudicates subjective disputes through staked guardians and a
multilevel appeal mechanism. Its appeal tree is a sophisticated refinement of
the simple single-round design we analyze, and could be layered onto our voting
contract; however, Aragon Court likewise lacks any linkage from verdicts to
guarantee pricing or to a persistent party reputation used for access control.

\paragraph{UMA \cite{uma}.}
UMA's optimistic oracle resolves \emph{objective} data questions via a proposer
bond and a challenge window, with a final staked token vote. Its focus is
price-oracle truthfulness rather than bilateral dispute resolution between
trading parties, and it maintains no per-agent reputation or guarantee product.
The proposer-bond/challenge structure is complementary to -- not a substitute
for -- the staked-community voting we analyze.

\paragraph{EAS \cite{eas}.}
Ethereum Attestation Service provides portable, verifiable attestations without
any adjudicative or staked-verdict layer. AgentTrust's \textsf{ReputationHub}
profile is conceptually an attestation of behavior, and the MVP records the same
information the full design would carry as EAS attestations; but EAS supplies no
mechanism by which a disputed outcome is \emph{decided}, and no incentive for a
dispute to be decided truthfully. Our contribution is precisely the missing
decision-and-incentive layer on top of attestable records.

\paragraph{Traditional arbitration.}
Classical arbitration is centralized: a designated authority holds exclusive
adjudication power, which our threat model identifies as T4 (single-point
authority abuse), and it lacks crypto-economic staking, on-chain verdict
enforcement, and reputation that prices future insurance. AgentTrust removes the
single point of authority (community supermajority), enforces the verdict
atomically with fund release (Definition~\ref{def:escrow}), and makes
reputation a storable economic asset.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The distinctive claim of this chapter is that \emph{no deployed system combines
all three elements}. Kleros and Aragon Court provide staked adjudication but no
reputation loop and no endogenous guarantee. UMA provides staked truthfulness
for oracles but no party reputation and no guarantee product. EAS provides
attestations but no adjudication incentive. Traditional arbitration provides
accountability but no decentralization. AgentTrust's Schelling-point reputation
community is, to our knowledge, the only mechanism in which the same community
stake that adjudicates a dispute simultaneously prices the guarantee of the
next trade and updates the reputation that gates admission -- adjudication,
guarantee, and reputation form one closed, incentive-compatible loop.
